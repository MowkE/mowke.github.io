Personal statement

Moledro

n.a feeling of resonant connection with an author or artist you'll never meet.

John Koenig created this term. He actually created an entire dictionary—The Dictionary of Obscure Sorrows—that is full of words he’s invented (“neologisms”). I found his work while doomscrolling in tenth grade, at a low point from AP week, deadlines for my research group, and facing general teen angst. Seeing his words, I found names for my understanding of the world.

Mimeoia

n.The frustration of knowing how neatly you embody a certain stereotype, without even intending to

After AP week, my math teacher assigned problems written for graduate students—ones that even my USAMO-qualifying friend struggled to solve. I was feeling bad about what I didn’t know, fearing being defined by a letter A-F (Minus, for some reason, E). I was in a state of “nullness”: instinctive restlessness that society had made choices on my behalf, like my own drive was outsourced. Fortunately, one stereotype I didn’t have was overbearing parents. Since they worked, I took the city bus everywhere, giving me the perfect opportunity to reclaim control over my life. So after school, I soon found myself wandering and discovering a secluded park. There was no tension. Just me, and a peculiar man who arrived.

Sonder

v.to recognize that everyone is the main character of their own stories.

He began doing a discount version of Tai Chi. Our unspoken vibe was that we were simply there to exist. Each afternoon, we saw each other, silently nodding. By the third week, I was doing Walmart Tai Chi, too. We talked. I’d never spoken with someone from a different generation and part of the world before. He told me everything: a creek in China that he escaped to with his girlfriend (now wife) to swim and fish with makeshift rods—which gave them food poisoning; taking trains to nearby cities and wandering night markets, leading to him opening a Bao shop after high school. He even brought me a red bean bao, which my desi tastebuds struggled with, but has become one of my favorite tastes now. Living inside this man’s life decentered me as the main character of mine. He made me believe that maybe I could wander beyond my mimeoia. Later, I went on a trip and haven’t seen him since.

I never got his name.

Silient

adj.related to the brilliant artistry hidden all around you

I met this man because I was initially going to go on a bus ride to nowhere. Without him, I finally followed through more. I started taking transit to San Francisco, walking around Pier 39, buying a one-way deck from the magic shop, and going to SF MoMA to appreciate the confusion of its images. They made me look at their meaning unbiased, and I was slowly seeing my own life without others’ biases, too.

I also took buses to San Jose State University, slipping into random lectures—on cell membrane thermodynamics, cell adhesion, and topics I barely grasped. But unlike in math, what I didn’t know excited me. Without the pressure of grades or performance, my ignorance became eagerness.

Maybe in dedication to the man at the park and our comparison of his East and my South Asian experiences, I took my school’s Asian Literature class. I thought frequently about his bao shop, and how he was a community hub. This made me increase my time guiding neurodivergent students at a center near my school, enjoying sharing time with my community.

Writing this essay makes me feel what John Koenig calls a “tiltshift”—a phenomenon where your lived experience seems oddly inconsequential once written down. I’m not sure if a college essay about my life should feel bigger than this, but I do know that thanks to John Koenig, I’ve gained words to understand my past while I learn to create my own new vocabulary for the future.
